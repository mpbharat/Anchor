How Anchor
actually works.

Anchor runs one loop every week. You put a few things on the record, it watches your real life against them, it pushes back when you drift, and it settles up on Friday. Saying no is the feature.

01
You commit
Three or four things for the week. By voice or text. Each one gets an if-then plan.
02
It watches
Your load, your inbox, your calendar. It knows what you took on and what is slipping.
03
It pushes back
A fifth thing, a purchase over budget, a Friday pattern. It nudges, or it says no.
04
It settles up
Friday recap. What you kept, what slipped, forgiven and carried. Then the loop resets.

Everything below is one of those four steps, in detail.

STEP 01 · THE FEW

A hard cap of four.

Most tools reward you for adding more. Anchor does the opposite. You can hold four commitments in a week and no more, because the limit is what makes them real. Little's Law, not willpower: fewer things in progress means things actually finish.

Each commitment carries a small if-then plan ("if it hits Friday, I draft it Thursday morning"). That single habit, implementation intentions, roughly doubles follow-through in the research. Anchor captures it in the same breath you commit.

STEP 03 · THE NO

It reasons over your week, then refuses.

When you try to take on something new, Anchor weighs it against what you are already carrying and what you have said matters. If you are full, it declines, and it does it the way a good coach would: it reflects the ask back, affirms it is your call, and names the trade you would be making. No guilt, no lecture. It uses your real numbers and never invents them.

STEP 02 · MEMORY

It learns how you overcommit.

After you talk with Anchor, a quiet reflection pass reads the conversation and updates what it knows about you: where you tend to overcommit, what you actually prioritise, your habits. It runs on a debounce, not on every message, so it stays cheap. The model proposes; the code reconciles and dedupes, so your memory sharpens instead of bloating. The more you talk, the better its "no" gets.

STEP 03 · NUDGES

It reads the signals, then nudges when it counts.

Anchor only interrupts when something real happens. Two kinds. Reactive, when a signal breaches a commitment right now. Proactive, when it sees a pattern about to repeat and gets ahead of it. Both land on your phone and your wrist.

REACTIVE · SOMETHING BREACHED

Gmail. An Amazon receipt lands and pushes you past your dining cap. "That is 1,050 of your 1,000 AED. Worth it?"
Calendar. A three-hour meeting drops onto your deep-work morning. "This collides with shipping the pricing revamp. Move it or drop it?"

THE DEFAULT

Silent unless it matters.

Anchor never interrupts you uninvited. When it decides there is nothing worth saying, it says nothing, and writes that decision down.
Open the silence log and you can see every moment it chose to stay quiet. It is the opposite of an app fighting for your attention, and it is the thing that earns the trust to say no when it counts.
HOW YOU TALK TO IT

A real conversation, out loud.

Anchor is a conversation first. Tap voice and it is a live, natural call: you think out loud, it answers in the moment, and when a factual question comes up ("is a half marathon in six weeks realistic?") it checks real sources before it answers instead of guessing. Coach, counsellor, friend, and the honest voice that still says the hard thing. One personality that flexes to what you need.

STEP 04 · THE RECAP

Friday, it settles up. Kindly.

At the end of the week Anchor shows where you stand: what you kept, what slipped. A miss is a miss, named plainly, then forgiven and carried, not turned into a shame spiral. Self-forgiveness after a slip is what predicts getting back on track, so the recap is built to forgive on purpose.

BASED ON THE SCIENCE

Every behaviour has a reason.

Anchor is not a productivity aesthetic. Each choice traces to a finding about how commitment and follow-through actually work.

Implementation intentions
Gollwitzer
An "if-then" plan roughly doubles follow-through. In Anchor: every commitment captures its own if-then the moment you make it.
WIP limits, Little's Law
Queueing theory
Fewer things in progress means things finish sooner. In Anchor: a hard cap of four, not an open backlog.
Motivational interviewing, reactance
Miller & Rollnick; Brehm
A command makes people dig in and overcommit more. In Anchor: the "no" reflects the ask and affirms it is your call instead of ordering you.
The planning fallacy
Kahneman & Tversky
We underestimate how long things take. In Anchor: it grounds "is this realistic?" in real sources before it agrees.
Self-forgiveness, the what-the-hell effect
Polivy & Herman; Wohl
Shame after a slip makes the next slip more likely; self-forgiveness predicts recovery. In Anchor: the recap forgives a miss and carries it, never piles on.
The fresh-start effect
Milkman
People re-commit at natural boundaries. In Anchor: the week is the unit, and each Friday resets the loop.
Overjustification
Deci
External rewards crowd out real motivation. In Anchor: no points, no streaks, no gamification. The only reward is keeping your word.
UNDER THE HOOD

Built to be trusted.

Your keys stay on the server, never in the app. Google is read-only: Anchor reads your calendar and inbox to see your load, and never sends, deletes, or posts anything. The reasoning runs on OpenAI, voice on OpenAI Realtime, grounding on Exa, all behind a Supabase backend.
